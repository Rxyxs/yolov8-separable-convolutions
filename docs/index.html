YOLOv8 with standard vs. separable convolutions on GPU, CPU and Raspberry Pi

Two YOLOv8 detectors trained from scratch in TensorFlow/Keras on COCO2017 (118,287 training images, 5,000 validation). Swapping Conv2D for SeparableConv2D costs 17% of mAP50 and changes almost nothing on a desktop, but on a Raspberry Pi it is the difference between one frame every 14 seconds and one every 1.5.

The problem in plain terms

An object detector looks at an image and returns a box and a label for every object it recognises: "person here, bicycle there". YOLO (You Only Look Once) does it in a single pass through a convolutional network, which is why it is the usual choice for real-time video: driver assistance, anti-collision systems, surveillance cameras, inspection on a production line.

The catch is cost. Most of the arithmetic in YOLO happens in convolution layers, and a model that runs smoothly on a gaming GPU can be unusable on a small, cheap, low-power board mounted on a vehicle or a camera, which is exactly where these systems need to live. This thesis asks a concrete question: if we replace YOLOv8's standard convolutions with a cheaper kind, how much accuracy do we lose and how much speed do we gain, and does the answer change with the hardware?

What the project set out to do

Objectives

  • Analyse the YOLOv8 architecture and the techniques it introduces over earlier YOLO versions.
  • Build two versions of the model from scratch: the classic one with Conv2D and a modified one with SeparableConv2D.
  • Measure both on accuracy, inference speed and computational efficiency.
  • Compare them on reduced hardware, a Raspberry Pi, against a desktop CPU and GPU.

Hypothesis

Deep learning models adapted to reduced hardware deliver more accuracy for the resources available than traditional models, measured on specific performance metrics (mAP, precision, speed, efficiency). The verdict is at the end of the page.

Separable convolutions are the trick behind MobileNet and Xception and have been tried on YOLOv3-Lite, YOLOv5 variants and YOLOv7's ELAN blocks. YOLOv8 does not use them out of the box, which is the gap this work tests.

Why a separable convolution is cheaper

A standard 3×3 convolution that turns Cin input channels into Cout output channels learns one 3×3 filter for every input–output pair: 9 · Cin · Cout weights. It looks at space (the 3×3 neighbourhood) and at channels (colour, edges, textures…) at the same time.

A depthwise separable convolution splits that into two cheaper steps:

  • Depthwise: one 3×3 filter per input channel, applied to that channel alone. It captures spatial patterns: 9 · Cin weights.
  • Pointwise: a 1×1 convolution that mixes the channels together: Cin · Cout weights.

With 128 channels in and out, that is 147,456 weights for the standard layer against 17,536 for the separable one, about 8× fewer, with the same drop in multiplications. The price is expressiveness: the network can no longer learn arbitrary combinations of "where" and "which channel" in a single step. Applied to the Head of YOLOv8, the whole model goes from 3.99 M to 1.26 M parameters.

How detection quality is measured

  • IoU (Intersection over Union): how much a predicted box overlaps the true one, from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (identical). A prediction counts as correct only if its IoU passes a threshold.
  • Precision: of the boxes the model drew, the share that were real objects. Low precision means false alarms.
  • Recall: of the real objects in the image, the share the model found. Low recall means missed objects.
  • mAP50: the area under the precision–recall curve, averaged over COCO's 80 classes, with a correct-box threshold of IoU ≥ 0.5. It is the headline number for comparing detectors.
  • FPS and time per image: how fast inference runs; the standard deviation of the time tells you how predictable that speed is, which matters for anything real-time.

How the experiment was run

A quantitative comparative design: both models go through exactly the same pipeline, so any difference comes from the architecture, not from the data or the evaluation.

  1. Implement YOLOv8 in Keras following the published architecture: Backbone with C2f blocks and SPPF, Neck with upsampling and concatenation across three scales, decoupled Head with separate box and class branches.
  2. Build the separable variant by replacing the Conv2D layers of the prediction Head with SeparableConv2D, leaving the feature extractor intact. Parameters drop from 3,991,584 to 1,258,715.
  3. Train both on COCO2017 with checkpoints after every epoch. At 10–12 hours per epoch this took weeks of GPU time, and the checkpoints are what made it survive power cuts and code failures without losing progress.
  4. Validate on the 5,000 COCO images: mAP50, mean IoU, precision, recall, and qualitative detections on the same images for both models.
  5. Measure inference on the desktop CPU, the desktop GPU and the Raspberry Pi, recording mean time per image and its standard deviation.

The three blocks of YOLOv8

  • Backbone, feature extraction: turns the image into feature maps at several resolutions.
  • Neck, multi-scale fusion: mixes fine spatial detail with coarse context so the model sees both small and large objects.
  • Head, predictions: for each of three scales, one branch regresses boxes and another classifies them. This is where the separable layers go.

The finding

The question was whether a lighter convolution buys enough speed to justify the accuracy it gives up. The answer depends entirely on the hardware:

  • On a desktop it does not pay. With an RTX 4060 Ti, or even on the Ryzen CPU alone, both models run at the same speed (5.4–5.5 FPS on GPU, 2.3–2.4 on CPU). The bottleneck there is data loading, host–GPU transfers and the TensorFlow runtime, not the parameter count, so the 68% reduction buys nothing and Conv2D keeps its quality edge.
  • On the Raspberry Pi it is decisive. Without a GPU, Conv2D needs 13.9 s per image and its latency swings by ±1.5 s; the separable model needs 1.48 s with ±0.01 s. Faster and stable, which is what a continuously running edge system needs.
  • The accuracy gap is architectural, not undertraining. The separable model got twice the epochs (100 vs. 50) and still finished below Conv2D on every quality metric.

Reading the table

  • Quality: Conv2D wins every quality metric. The biggest gap is in recall (0.326 vs. 0.293): the separable model mostly misses objects rather than drawing wrong boxes, since precision and IoU are nearly tied. When it finds something, it locates it almost as well.
  • Desktop speed: a 68% smaller model runs at the same speed. Fewer multiplications only help if multiplications are the bottleneck; on a modern GPU they are not, the time goes into moving images to the GPU and into framework overhead. Separable convolutions are also less efficient per operation on GPUs, which eats the remaining theoretical advantage.
  • Raspberry Pi speed: here every multiplication runs on a 1.5 GHz ARM CPU and memory bandwidth is scarce, so the 8× cheaper layers translate almost directly into speed: 9.4× more FPS. The variance matters as much: Conv2D's latency jumps between roughly 12 and 15 s per frame, the separable model is steady to the hundredth of a second.

What changes in the architecture

A standard convolution filters space and channels at once. A depthwise separable one splits that into a per-channel spatial filter (depthwise) followed by a 1×1 channel mix (pointwise), the same idea behind MobileNet and Xception. Here the swap is applied to the prediction Head; the backbone is unchanged.

Choosing the number of epochs

Each epoch over COCO2017, training plus validation, took 10–12 hours on the RTX 4060 Ti, so the epoch budget was chosen by checking mAP every 10 epochs. Conv2D peaks at epoch 50 and then degrades (overfitting). The separable model converges more slowly and noisily, settling around epoch 100.

Detections side by side

Same validation images, ground truth in red and predictions in yellow. Conv2D keeps tighter, more stable boxes on small and medium objects; the separable model misses more of them, which is where its lower recall comes from.

Getting it onto the Raspberry Pi

Exporting to TensorFlow Lite was not a one-liner. DepthwiseConv2dNative is not among TFLite's built-in ops, so the export needed Select TF Ops, and the graph had to be restructured: pure inference was split from the label encoder and from non-maximum suppression, keeping only the layers needed for prediction. Part of the graph then runs as Flex ops that miss TFLite/XNNPACK optimizations. That hurts the heavier Conv2D graph most, which explains part of its slow and erratic latency on the Pi.

Limitations

  • Unequal epochs (50 vs. 100). It favours the separable model, so the quality gap is if anything understated.
  • Low absolute mAP. Both models were trained from scratch with a compute budget measured in weeks, without a hyperparameter search; they are far below Ultralytics' pretrained YOLOv8 and are meant to compare the two convolutions, not to compete on COCO.
  • Pi latencies depend on the TFLite conversion path. Read them as relative between models, not as absolute numbers for any deployment.
  • One dataset, two platforms. Hardware with dedicated accelerators (Jetson, Edge TPU, NPUs) could change the trade-off; that is the natural next step.

The hypothesis, checked

Partly confirmed. In absolute terms the adapted model is less accurate: 0.175 vs. 0.212 mAP50, and no amount of extra epochs closed the gap. Relative to the resources it uses, it wins clearly on the device it was designed for: on the Raspberry Pi it delivers 83% of the accuracy at roughly a tenth of the latency and a third of the size, while the standard model is not usable there at all (one frame every 14 s). On a desktop the trade-off does not exist, because the lighter model is not faster.

What comes next

  • Dedicated edge accelerators: Google Edge TPU, NVIDIA Jetson, FPGAs and NPUs, where depthwise operations are supported natively.
  • Lightweight baselines: compare against detectors built for the edge, such as NanoDet, MobileNet-SSD and PP-YOLO.
  • Recovering accuracy: fine-tuning, stronger augmentation, quantization or neural architecture search for the separable model.
  • Field deployment: latency, energy and stability measured in a real application, not on the validation set.

When to use which

If the model runs on a desktop or server GPU, use Conv2D: you keep the accuracy and pay nothing in speed. If it runs on a CPU-only embedded device, use the separable variant: 17% less mAP50 buys a model that is about 10× faster, 3× smaller and has predictable latency, which matters as much as accuracy for anti-collision or continuous monitoring.
