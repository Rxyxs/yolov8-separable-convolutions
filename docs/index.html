YOLOv8 with standard vs. separable convolutions on GPU, CPU and Raspberry Pi

Two YOLOv8 detectors trained from scratch in TensorFlow/Keras on COCO2017 (118,287 training images, 5,000 validation). Swapping Conv2D for SeparableConv2D costs 17% of mAP50 and changes almost nothing on a desktop, but on a Raspberry Pi it is the difference between one frame every 14 seconds and one every 1.5.

The finding

The question was whether a lighter convolution buys enough speed to justify the accuracy it gives up. The answer depends entirely on the hardware:

  • On a desktop it does not pay. With an RTX 4060 Ti, or even on the Ryzen CPU alone, both models run at the same speed (5.4–5.5 FPS on GPU, 2.3–2.4 on CPU). The bottleneck there is data loading, host–GPU transfers and the TensorFlow runtime, not the parameter count, so the 68% reduction buys nothing and Conv2D keeps its quality edge.
  • On the Raspberry Pi it is decisive. Without a GPU, Conv2D needs 13.9 s per image and its latency swings by ±1.5 s; the separable model needs 1.48 s with ±0.01 s. Faster and stable, which is what a continuously running edge system needs.
  • The accuracy gap is architectural, not undertraining. The separable model got twice the epochs (100 vs. 50) and still finished below Conv2D on every quality metric.

What changes in the architecture

A standard convolution filters space and channels at once. A depthwise separable one splits that into a per-channel spatial filter (depthwise) followed by a 1×1 channel mix (pointwise), the same idea behind MobileNet and Xception. Here the swap is applied to the Neck's multi-scale fusion blocks and the Head's prediction branches; the backbone is unchanged.

Choosing the number of epochs

Each epoch over COCO2017, training plus validation, took 10–12 hours on the RTX 4060 Ti, so the epoch budget was chosen by checking mAP every 10 epochs. Conv2D peaks at epoch 50 and then degrades (overfitting). The separable model converges more slowly and noisily, settling around epoch 100.

Detections side by side

Same validation images, ground truth in red and predictions in yellow. Conv2D keeps tighter, more stable boxes on small and medium objects; the separable model misses more of them, which is where its lower recall comes from.

Getting it onto the Raspberry Pi

Exporting to TensorFlow Lite was not a one-liner. DepthwiseConv2dNative is not among TFLite's built-in ops, so the export needed Select TF Ops, and the graph had to be restructured: pure inference was split from the label encoder and from non-maximum suppression, keeping only the layers needed for prediction. Part of the graph then runs as Flex ops that miss TFLite/XNNPACK optimizations. That hurts the heavier Conv2D graph most, which explains part of its slow and erratic latency on the Pi.

Limitations

  • Unequal epochs (50 vs. 100). It favours the separable model, so the quality gap is if anything understated.
  • Low absolute mAP. Both models were trained from scratch with a compute budget measured in weeks, without a hyperparameter search; they are far below Ultralytics' pretrained YOLOv8 and are meant to compare the two convolutions, not to compete on COCO.
  • Pi latencies depend on the TFLite conversion path. Read them as relative between models, not as absolute numbers for any deployment.
  • One dataset, two platforms. Hardware with dedicated accelerators (Jetson, Edge TPU, NPUs) could change the trade-off; that is the natural next step.

When to use which

If the model runs on a desktop or server GPU, use Conv2D: you keep the accuracy and pay nothing in speed. If it runs on a CPU-only embedded device, use the separable variant: 17% less mAP50 buys a model that is about 10× faster, 3× smaller and has predictable latency, which matters as much as accuracy for anti-collision or continuous monitoring.
